# 13 Meshing: feature-preserving extraction

M0 clean-room note, 2026-10-15. Own words from the papers below (read as papers). No third-party code opened or copied.

## What it must do (brief, design instruction 11 and test A)
Our own feature-preserving extraction from the SDF, with resolution adapted to curvature, relief importance and on-screen size per tier. Meshes must be watertight, manifold, free of self-intersections and within triangle budgets.

## Known approaches
- **Marching Cubes** (Lorensen and Cline 1987): per-cell lookup of triangles from corner signs, vertices on edges. Simple; rounds off sharp edges and corners; has ambiguous cases that can create holes unless resolved consistently.
- **Extended Marching Cubes** (Kobbelt et al. 2001): adds a vertex inside cells that contain a sharp feature, found from the surface normals.
- **Dual Contouring of Hermite Data** (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer, Warren 2002): store, for each cell edge the surface crosses, the crossing point and the surface normal there (Hermite data). Put one vertex per cell at the point that best fits all the tangent planes of its edges (a small least-squares problem, the quadric error function, solved stably with a truncated singular value decomposition, pulled towards the average of the crossing points when the planes do not pin a point). Connect the vertices of the four cells around each crossed edge into a quad. Reproduces sharp edges and corners and works on adaptive octrees, which it can simplify by merging cells whose fit error is small. Known weaknesses: one vertex per cell can join separate surface sheets into non-manifold geometry, and the best-fit vertex can leave its cell and cause self-intersections.
- **Successors.** Dual Marching Cubes (Schaefer and Warren 2004) contours on the dual grid of the octree and keeps sharp features with fewer artefacts; Manifold Dual Contouring (Schaefer, Ju and Warren 2007) allows several vertices per cell and only merges cells when the result stays manifold; intersection-free contouring on octrees (Ju and Udeshi 2006) constrains vertices to avoid crossings; Surface Nets (Gibson 1998) places a smoothed vertex per cell without feature handling; learned variants (Neural Dual Contouring, Chen et al. 2022; Occupancy-Based Dual Contouring, Hwang and Sung 2024) improve vertex placement for learned or occupancy fields.

## Our approach (a dual method of our own, using what the part graph knows)
- **Adaptive octree from the field.** Cells are subdivided where the surface is (Lipschitz culling, note 05) and where it matters, by a priority that combines: curvature (change of the exact gradient across the cell), relief importance (a geometry relief layer present at this tier, note 12), on-screen size at the tier's typical view distance (cells smaller than about one pixel stop), part importance from the template (wheels and silhouette parts before hidden underside), and silhouette contribution for the class's typical views (note 14). Refinement stops when the tier's triangle budget is reached; the priority queue is processed in a fixed order so the result is deterministic.
- **Exact Hermite data.** Edge crossings are found by a fixed number of bracketed root-finding steps on the field (bisection then secant, fixed count), and the normal is the exact gradient from the CPU evaluator, never a finite difference.
- **Vertex placement with graph hints.** Each cell's vertex comes from the tangent-plane fit solved by our own 3x3 symmetric eigen-decomposition (fixed number of Jacobi sweeps, deterministic), with truncation of small eigenvalues and a pull towards the mean crossing point. The part graph tells us where sharp features should exist: a generator declares its crease curves (a box with a bevel smaller than the cell, a blade's edge). Away from declared creases the pull is stronger, so smooth toon shapes never grow spurious spikes; at declared creases the fit is trusted.
- **Manifold by construction.** A cell whose crossings form more than one separate surface patch (found from its corner and edge signs, plus a centre sample for ambiguous faces) gets one vertex per patch, and the quad builder connects each edge to the vertex of the matching patch, so separate sheets never fuse. Octree simplification merges cells only when the merged cell has one patch and the fit error is under the tier's tolerance.
- **No self-intersections: the checker is the guarantee.** Each vertex is clamped to its cell (shrunk by a small margin), which keeps triangles from distant cells apart but does not by itself stop neighbouring quads from crossing (intersection-free contouring needs more than clamping). So after extraction every tier's mesh goes through our own mesh checker, and any issue triggers local refinement of the affected cells and re-extraction, never a silent pass.
- **Our own mesh checker (`meshcheck`, decision D17).** A small module of evani-model with no dependencies beyond the standard library: it takes plain arrays (positions, triangle indices, optional part ids) and returns a sorted list of issues with the triangles involved. Checks: degenerate and duplicate triangles, open and non-manifold edges, flipped normals (orientation against neighbours and the field's gradient), and self-intersections. It does not use geocheck's checker, because geocheck depends on evani-core and evani-render (its Cargo.toml has them unconditionally) and its self-intersection test compares pairs inside a box prefilter, which grows with the square of the triangle count (docs/GEOCHECK.md). Ours finds candidate pairs through a uniform spatial grid sized from the mean edge length (each triangle registered in the cells its box touches, pairs tested once, in cell order), so a 60k-triangle Ultra kart costs about the triangle count times a small constant; pairs that share a vertex or an edge are tested for crossing beyond the shared part only. Edge maps use sorted vectors, not hash maps, so the issue list is identical on every machine and thread count. Budget: under 5% of a kart's compile time (measured in M1, task T9). It is designed so that geocheck could later use the same code (moved to a tiny shared crate), which is Chris's call (D17); until then the benchmark tool cross-checks both checkers on every benchmark mesh, and any disagreement is a bug in one of them.
- **Watertight.** The octree's root box encloses the whole model with margin, so every surface is closed.
- **Quad splitting.** Each quad is split along the diagonal whose midpoint field value is closer to zero (ties broken by index), which keeps creases straight.
- **Thin features.** A wall or rod thinner than two finest cells triggers extra refinement; thinner than the finest allowed cell, the generator's explicit mesh is used for that part (brief: honest limits) and joined to the rest by the contact rule.

## Why it should be better
- Exact gradients and fixed-step root finding give clean Hermite data, which is what DC needs most.
- Creases are known from generators, not guessed from normals, so we keep the sharp edges that are meant to be sharp and smooth everything else: the main source of DC artefacts (spikes, kinks) is avoided by knowledge, not by heuristics.
- Resolution goes where the game's camera and the tier need it, so triangle budgets buy visible quality.

## Baseline and measure
- Test A: watertight, manifold, no self-intersections on every benchmark model and tier; triangle count within budget.
- Accuracy: Hausdorff and Chamfer distance from the mesh to the field's true surface (sampled exactly), per triangle count, compared with plain marching cubes at the same triangle count (our own simple implementation as a control) and with meshoptimizer's simplifier applied to a fine marching-cubes mesh (run separately, note 14).
- Speed: kart-sized compile under 10 s Standard, 30 s Lite, 60 s CPU-only (test E).

## Risks
- **Multi-patch cells near tight blends** can still produce slivers. Mitigation: refinement first, slivers removed by a final validator pass with local re-extraction.
- **Determinism across thread counts.** Mitigation: fixed tiling and tile-ordered merging (note 18).
